@unit 10 | Introduction II: The criterion and experience | 81-89
@title Introduction II: The Criterion, Experience, and What Happens “Behind the Back” of Consciousness

@where
Hegel has said that the book will present knowledge as it appears, a path of despair driven by determinate negation (¶76–80). Now he explains precisely how this examination can work without importing a standard from outside: the method of the whole book.

@guide
**The question this section answers.** How can we examine whether consciousness really knows, if we may not presuppose any standard of truth? And what exactly happens when consciousness discovers that its knowledge does not fit its object?

**What Hegel does.** He states the problem: any examination needs a standard, but at the start nothing has been justified as the standard (¶81). The solution lies in the structure of consciousness itself (¶82–84). Consciousness distinguishes its *knowing* of an object (how the object is *for it*) from the object as it is *in itself* (its *truth*). So it carries its own standard: it can compare what it takes the object to be with how it knows it. We need bring no standard of our own, and even the comparison is done by consciousness itself; “all that remains for us is simply to look on” (¶85). When knowledge and object fail to match, both change: the standard itself is tested (¶85). This movement, in which a new true object arises, is *experience* (¶86). What consciousness does not see is how the new object arises from the old; that happens “behind its back”, for us, and is what makes the series necessary (¶87). So the path to science is itself science: the science of the experience of consciousness (¶88–89).

**Watch for.** The **problem of the criterion**; *for consciousness* versus *in itself*; **knowing** and **truth**; **concept** and **object** (in two directions); “**simply to look on**”; the **standard alters**; **experience** as the arising of a new object; **the reversal of consciousness**; **behind the back of consciousness**; “**for us**”; **shapes of consciousness**.

**How to read it.** These are the most important methodological paragraphs of the book. Read ¶84–87 at least twice, and come back to them after Sensory Certainty (¶90–110), when you have seen the method at work.

@map
thesis: Consciousness contains its own standard of truth, so it can examine itself; when its knowledge fails the test, a new object and a new shape arise, and the necessary sequence of these is the science of the experience of consciousness.
target: The assumption that examining knowledge requires an external standard (and so either dogmatism, regress or circularity: the sceptic’s “problem of the criterion”).
step: An examination needs a standard taken as the in-itself; at the outset none has been justified (¶81).
step: Consciousness relates to something (knowing) and distinguishes it as being in itself (truth) (¶82).
step: If we examine knowledge, its truth becomes our knowledge of it, and the standard would be ours, not its own (¶83).
step: But consciousness provides its own standard: it compares its knowing (concept) with what it takes to be the in-itself (object), so we need import nothing (¶84).
step: Consciousness itself carries out the comparison; when the two do not correspond, knowledge alters, the object alters too, and the standard itself is tested (¶85).
step: This dialectical movement, from which a new true object emerges, is experience; the new object is the being-for-consciousness of the first in-itself (¶86).
step: The emergence of the new object, a reversal of consciousness, is “our contribution”; it goes on behind the back of consciousness and makes the sequence necessary (¶87).
conclusion: The path to science is itself science, the science of the experience of consciousness, whose moments are shapes of consciousness; it ends where appearance equals essence (¶88–89).
stakes: Whether an immanent critique, one that measures a view only by its own standard, can lead anywhere determinate; and who “we” are.

@friendly Measuring a promise against itself
Suppose someone tells you: “I am a completely honest person.” You do not need a philosophy of honesty to test this. You simply watch whether their actions meet the standard *they themselves* set. If they lie, the claim fails by its own measure.

Hegel does the same with ways of knowing. Each one announces what knowledge is (“to know is to see things directly”, “to know is to grasp the laws behind appearances”) and what its objects are. So it carries its own yardstick. We just watch whether it lives up to it. And here is the twist: when a way of knowing fails its own test, it is not left empty. Its failure shows what it was *really* dealing with, and that becomes the starting point of a new way of knowing. The person who claimed perfect honesty may learn, through failing, what honesty actually demands.

@c 81 | objection | hegel
why: Hegel states the ancient “problem of the criterion” (Heidemann traces it to Agrippa’s trilemma in Sextus Empiricus): any test needs a criterion, which must itself be tested, and so on, unless we argue in a circle or simply assume it. If Hegel used his own philosophy as the standard, he would beg the question against natural consciousness.

@l 81 | the method of carrying it out
So far Hegel has explained why the journey is necessary and where it goes (¶76–80). Now he turns to how it is to be carried out: the method.

@l 81 | examination of the reality of cognition
The book is science’s response to knowledge as it appears: it examines whether that knowledge really is knowledge.

@l 81 | adopted as a standard underlying it
But every examination seems to need a presupposition, some standard against which the thing examined is measured.

@l 81 | agreement or disagreement of it with the standard
That is simply what testing is. A coin is tested against a known weight; an answer against the right answer. Whether it passes depends on whether it agrees with the standard.

@l 81 | as the essence or as the in-itself
Whatever serves as the standard is thereby treated as what is true in itself, the measure of everything else. That would hold even if the standard were science, Hegel’s own philosophy.

@l 81 | neither science itself nor anything else has justified itself
And here is the problem: at the start of the book nothing has yet earned the right to be the standard, not even science. Using it anyway would beg the question (see ¶76).

@l 81 | no examination can take place
So it seems no examination is possible at all. This is the ancient “problem of the criterion” (Sextus Empiricus): to test any claim you need a criterion, which must itself be tested, and so on without end, unless you argue in a circle or simply assume one.

@c 82 | definition | hegel
why: The simplest structure of consciousness as it understands itself: I am aware of something (it is *for me*), and I take that something to exist on its own account (it is *in itself*). Houlgate stresses that Hegel is not giving his own theory of mind here, but describing how consciousness takes itself to be.

@l 82 | as they occur in consciousness
To solve the problem, Hegel first recalls two basic features of how consciousness understands its own knowing. He calls them abstract determinations because they are bare and general.

@l 82 | at the same time relates itself
Feature one: consciousness distinguishes something from itself and, in the same act, relates itself to it. To be aware of a tree is to take the tree as other than me, and yet to be in contact with it.

@l 82 | is knowing
Hegel’s name for this relation is “knowing”: the object’s being there for consciousness, as known.

@l 82 | is called truth
Feature two: consciousness also takes the object to exist outside this relation, on its own account, not merely as known. That side, the object in itself, is what it calls “truth”.

@l 82 | of no further concern to us here
Hegel will not now give a theory of what knowing and truth really are. He takes them as consciousness itself presents them, because the book’s object is knowledge as it appears.

@l 82 | very much as we have conceived them
A reassurance: this is how ordinary consciousness does understand itself. Hegel is not slipping in a theory of his own.

@g 82 | for consciousness
How the object is known or appears to consciousness: the object in its relation to the knower. Hegel calls this side “knowing”.

@g 82 | Being-in-itself
How the object is taken to be apart from its relation to consciousness: what it really is. Hegel calls this side “truth”. Note that consciousness itself makes this distinction.

@lens 82 | Wissen, Gewissheit, Bewusstsein
Three words of one family run through the book. *Wissen* is knowing or knowledge; *Gewissheit* is certainty, literally “sure-knownness”; *Bewusstsein*, consciousness, is literally “being-known-ness” or “being-aware”. In German one hears that consciousness *is* a kind of knowing, and that certainty is knowing that is sure of itself.

The pairing to watch is *Gewissheit* and *Wahrheit*, certainty and truth. Every shape of consciousness begins with a *certainty* (what it is sure it knows) and tests it against what it takes as the *truth* (how things are in themselves). The chapters are titled accordingly: “Sense-certainty”, and Self-consciousness as “the truth of the certainty of oneself”. The book is the story of certainty learning its truth.

@c 83 | objection | hegel
why: The problem sharpened: an outside observer who tests consciousness by the observer’s own standards has not tested consciousness at all, merely compared it with the observer. Consciousness could reject the verdict as one more bare claim (¶76).

@l 83 | what knowledge is in itself
Now the problem sharpens. To investigate the truth of knowledge is to ask what knowledge is in itself, apart from how anyone takes it.

@l 83 | rather its Being for us
But in such an investigation, knowledge is our object: it is “for us”. So whatever we found it to be “in itself” would really be how it appears to us, the investigators.

@l 83 | only our knowledge of it
Hence what we declared to be its essence would not be its truth, only our knowledge of it: the same gap between appearance and reality, now opening between us and the knowledge we examine.

@l 83 | would not necessarily have to recognize the standard
Worse, the standard would lie in us, and the consciousness under examination could simply reject it as ours, not its own: one bare claim against another again (¶76). An outside test settles nothing.

@c 84 | thesis | hegel
why: Hegel’s answer to the problem of the criterion, and the reason the book is an *immanent* critique: each shape is judged by its own standard. The double formulation (concept to object, object to concept) prepares a subtle point: the examination may show not only that knowing must change, but that the object was not what consciousness took it to be.

@l 84 | this semblance of separation and presupposition
Here comes the solution. The problem of ¶81–83 rested on separating the standard from what is tested. The nature of consciousness removes that separation, which was only a seeming one.

@l 84 | a comparison of consciousness with itself
Consciousness carries its own standard. So the examination need not bring in an outside measure: it is consciousness compared with itself.

@l 84 | falls within it
Why? Because the distinction just made in ¶82, between knowing and truth, falls within consciousness itself. Both the measure and what is measured are already there.

@l 84 | the moment of truth
Not the English idiom. A “moment”, for Hegel, is an aspect or element of a whole. Consciousness has two moments: knowledge (the object as it is for consciousness) and truth (the object as consciousness takes it to be in itself, outside that relation).

@l 84 | by which to measure its knowledge
So whatever consciousness itself declares to be the true, the in-itself, is the yardstick, and its knowledge is what gets measured against it. The standard is consciousness’s own, so it cannot reject the verdict.

@l 84 | whether the concept corresponds to the object
One way to put the test: call the knowing the “concept” and the true the “object”, and ask whether our concept fits the object, as in ordinary checking: does my idea of the tree fit the tree?

@l 84 | whether the object corresponds to its concept
The reverse way: call what the object essentially is its “concept”, and call the object as it shows up for consciousness the “object”. Then we ask whether the object lives up to its concept, as we ask whether a friend is a true friend.

@l 84 | these amount to the same thing
Both formulations describe one comparison, run from opposite ends. The reverse version matters later: sometimes it is the object, not our knowledge, that fails to live up to its concept.

@l 84 | we do not need to import standards
The essential point: both sides of the comparison fall within the knowledge being examined, so we need not import our own standards. This is what makes the critique *immanent*, judged from within.

@l 84 | by leaving these aside
By setting aside our own notions, the observers can consider the matter as it is in and for itself. Hegel’s method is a kind of disciplined restraint: let consciousness test itself.

@u 84 | Consciousness carries its own yardstick
step: To test any claim to knowledge, it seems we need a standard of truth. But which one? Any standard we bring would be our own, and could be rejected by what we are testing. Hegel’s insight: consciousness already has a standard *inside* it. Whenever it knows something, it takes that thing to be a certain way *in itself*, and it has a certain way of knowing it. So we can ask whether its knowing matches what it itself takes the object to be. The verdict is then binding on consciousness, because the standard is its own.

example: A scientist who claims her theory explains every observation can be tested by her own standard: find an observation it does not explain. Or a judge who says the law must treat like cases alike can be tested by finding like cases he treated differently. In both cases the critic needs no theory of his own; the claimant’s own standard does the work.

misreading: This does not mean consciousness is always right, or that truth is whatever consciousness thinks. It means that consciousness can be shown wrong *by its own lights*. Nor is consciousness’s standard fixed: it can turn out that the standard itself was inadequate (¶85).

returns: You will see the method at once: sensory certainty takes its object to be the immediate, singular *this*, and claims to know it immediately; testing this claim by its own standard is the whole of ¶90–110. Every chapter follows the same pattern, as the Shape at a Glance cards show.

@arg 84 | Hegel’s answer to the problem of the criterion
P1: To examine a claim to knowledge we need a standard; an external standard begs the question against the claim examined (¶81–83).
P2: Every consciousness distinguishes its knowing of an object from what it takes the object to be in itself (¶82).
P3: So every consciousness has, within itself, a standard (what it takes the object to be) against which its knowing can be measured.
C: Consciousness can be examined by its own standard, without any external presupposition; the examination is consciousness comparing itself with itself.
weak: Inwood notes a puzzle: consciousness “cannot get behind the scenes” to the object in itself (¶85). How can it compare its knowledge with something it can only know through that knowledge? Houlgate answers that the comparison is between the object as initially conceived and what experience shows it to be: the test happens *over time*, through experience.

@c 85 | argument | hegel
why: The most important paragraph of the Introduction. Two claims. First, our role is “pure looking on” (*das reine Zusehen*): consciousness tests itself. Second, the test is not like correcting a mistake about an object that stays the same; when knowledge changes, the object changes with it, because the object was always the object *as so conceived*. So the very idea of what is “in itself” is revised.

@l 85 | get behind the scenes
The sceptic’s worry: we only ever know the object as we know it, so how can we check our knowledge against the object as it really is? Hegel answers in the next sentence.

@l 85 | This differentiation is at hand
Consciousness already distinguishes the object it takes as real from its knowledge of it, so it need not step outside itself to compare them. Houlgate: the comparison happens over time, as experience shows the object to be other than it was taken to be.

@l 85 | the object itself alters for it too
The knowledge was knowledge of the object as so conceived, so correcting the knowledge changes what the object is taken to be. There is no unchanged object left to return to.

@l 85 | the standard of the assessment alters
The yardstick fails along with what it measured: each shape tests not only its beliefs but its idea of what counts as true.

@u 85 | When knowledge changes, the object changes too
step: In ordinary life, when I find I was wrong, I correct my belief and the object stays put: I thought it was a squirrel, it was a cat. Hegel says the experience of consciousness is different. A shape of consciousness is not wrong about this or that fact; it is wrong about what *kind* of thing an object is and what knowing it involves. When that conception collapses, there is no unchanged object to go back to: what it took to be the object “in itself” turns out to have been only its own conception. So the standard is revised along with the knowledge.

example: Houlgate’s example: I take the animal on the lawn to be a squirrel and then see it is a cat; that is ordinary correction. Compare this with discovering that the “objects” you had been taking as simple, self-contained things are really processes, or that what you took as a fixed quantity (absolute time) depends on the observer (as relativity showed). You do not just correct a belief; your notion of what the object *is* changes, and with it your notion of what counts as knowing it.

misreading: Hegel is not saying that the world changes when we change our minds, or that truth is subjective. The “object” here is the object *as a shape of consciousness conceives it*, the form of objectivity it takes for granted. That conception is what changes, irreversibly, because it has been shown not to hold up.

returns: In Sensory Certainty the object changes from “this, here, now” to “what I mean by this” and then to a “simple complex” of many nows, until a new object, the thing with properties, emerges (¶90–111). Houlgate calls these steps the “micro-transitions” within a shape.

@g 85 | simply to look on
German *das reine Zusehen*, “pure looking on”: the role of the philosophical observer, who need not intervene because consciousness tests itself. Houlgate notes that the observer is not wholly passive: “we” name and order what consciousness experiences.

@c 86 | definition | hegel
why: Hegel’s technical definition of experience. The new object is not found by chance; it is “what the first object turned out to be in being known”. Inwood’s gloss is helpful: the tension arises between what consciousness takes the object to be and how it actually knows it; the *way of knowing* then becomes the next object. Note the first use of “dialectical” in the body of the book.

@l 86 | the ambiguity of this truth
The object is “in itself”, but it is in itself *for consciousness*, so it has two sides: what it is taken to be, and how it is known. This doubleness drives experience.

@l 86 | the Being-for-consciousness of this in-itself
The object as consciousness actually knows it. At first this looks like mere reflection on one’s own knowing; Hegel’s point is that it becomes the new object.

@l 86 | contains the nullity of the first
The new object carries the failure of the old one within it: it is defined by what the old object turned out not to be. Determinate negation at work (¶79).

@g 86 | dialectical
From the Greek for conversation. For Hegel, the movement in which a determination, pursued on its own terms, turns into its opposite and so gives rise to something new. Here it is the self-examination of consciousness.

@c 87 | argument | hegel
why: The famous distinction between what is “for consciousness” and what is “for us”. The shape we observe experiences the collapse of its object and simply finds itself with a new one; it does not know where the new one came from. We see the whole movement and its necessity. Houlgate’s gloss: the *micro*-transitions within a shape are experienced by consciousness; the *macro*-transition to a new shape is made by us.

@l 87 | by chance and externally
Ordinary experience: I take the animal for a squirrel, then see it is a cat; I learn from a new object I happen upon. In the *Phenomenology* the new object grows out of the old one.

@l 87 | our contribution
The observers’ part: seeing that the new object arises from the old. This insight turns a series of experiences into a necessary sequence, a science.

@l 87 | behind the back of consciousness
The shape under study lives through the change but does not see how it came about; only we see the connection. See the Pause and Unpack box below.

@l 87 | only its formal aspect
We add no content of our own: what appears is what consciousness itself experiences. We grasp only the connection, the “pure emergence” of one object from another.

@g 87 | reversal of consciousness
German *Umkehrung des Bewusstseins*: the turning-around in which consciousness’s way of knowing its old object becomes its new object. It is “our contribution”: we see it; consciousness does not.

@u 87 | “Behind the back of consciousness”
step: Two perspectives run through the whole book. The consciousness being examined lives through its experience: it is sure of its object, finds that the object does not hold, and ends up with a new object, which it takes simply as how things are. It does not see the connection between the old object and the new. We, the observers, see that the new object is precisely what the old one turned out to be, and so we see the series as a necessary progression. The necessity is “for us”; the content is “for consciousness”.

example: Think of a person who goes through a series of relationships and each time says “this one is completely different”. A friend watching from outside sees the pattern: each new partner answers exactly what failed in the last. The person lives the content; the friend sees the logic. Psychoanalysts would say the pattern works “behind the back” of the person.

misreading: “Behind its back” does not mean that Hegel cheats by adding his own ideas, or that consciousness is stupid. Each shape is limited by definition: if it saw everything we see, it would not be the shape it is (Inwood). And “we” are not all-knowing gods; we only render explicit what the experience of consciousness implies.

returns: Watch for phrases like “for us” or “we have seen” throughout the chapters, marking where Hegel speaks as the observer. Example: at the end of Force and Understanding, “it is only for us that this truth exists, not yet for consciousness” (¶164–165). The commentary’s “Voice” tag tracks these shifts for you.

@c 88 | thesis | hegel
gist: Because of this necessity, the path to science is itself already science, and in its content the science of the experience of consciousness.

why: One sentence that gave the book its first title: *Science of the Experience of Consciousness*. Heidemann objects that this is where Hegel’s argument becomes circular: the necessity seen by “us” already presupposes the philosophical standpoint the path was supposed to justify (see the Objections box).

@c 89 | preview | hegel
why: The Introduction ends with the destination. Notice “shapes of consciousness” (*Gestalten des Bewusstseins*): each stage of the book is a whole way of being conscious, with its own object, its own certainty and its own experience. The end point is where consciousness no longer faces anything alien.

@l 89 | burdened with alien material
At every stage so far consciousness has faced something other than itself that it had to take in. At the end it finds that nothing it confronts is simply alien to it.

@l 89 | the appearance becomes equal to the essence
How things appear to consciousness and what they are no longer come apart. This is the standpoint of absolute knowing, where the *Phenomenology* ends and the system proper begins.

@g 89 | shapes of consciousness
German *Gestalten des Bewusstseins*: whole configurations of experience, each defined by what it takes the object to be, how it claims to know it, and what it is certain of. The chapters of the book are a sequence of such shapes.

@cross Who are “we”?
**The philosopher and the reader** (Houlgate): “we” are Hegel and those who follow his book. Hegel must suspend his own philosophical convictions; readers come with their natural certainties. Both watch consciousness, and the readers are educated by watching.

**The fully developed standpoint** (Heidemann; older readers): “we” already stand at the end of the journey, which is why we can see the necessity of each transition. This makes the book an exposition from the standpoint of absolute knowing, and raises the circularity problem.

**A community of reasoners** (Pinkard, Pippin): “we” are the modern community of those who demand reasons for every claim, the heirs of the Enlightenment for whom the book is written. The “we” anticipates “the I that is We” of Spirit (¶177).

**Your task.** Note the first appearance of “we” in Sensory Certainty (¶91–95). Is it doing work that consciousness could not do itself? Does it bring in anything beyond what the experience implies?

@obj Heidemann’s circularity objection
objection: Hegel claims to justify the standpoint of science without presupposing it. But the necessity of the sequence exists only “for us” (¶87), and “we” can see it only from the philosophical standpoint: “the path to science is itself already science” (¶88). The conclusion is used to make sense of the premises. Dietmar Heidemann (in Moyar & Quante) concludes that the *Phenomenology* fails as a theory of epistemic justification, though individual arguments within it may succeed.

reply: Houlgate replies that “we” contribute only the *formal* recognition of what consciousness’s own experience produces; “we” do not supply content, and the phenomenologist suspends his philosophical views. The transitions are made necessary by what emerges in consciousness itself; our role is to render it explicit. On this view the circle is not vicious: the end is confirmed by the path, not presupposed by it.

verdict: Test it yourself. At the transition from Sensory Certainty to Perception (¶109–111), does Hegel need anything that sensory certainty’s own experience has not provided?

@now When paradigms change, the facts change
Thomas Kuhn argued in *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) that when scientists change paradigms, they do not simply correct beliefs about a fixed world: what counts as an object, a problem, even a fact, changes too. After Lavoisier, chemists did not just correct their views about phlogiston; there was no phlogiston to have views about. Kuhn compared this to a “gestalt switch”.

Hegel’s claim that “in the alteration of the knowledge, the object itself alters” (¶85) is a philosophical ancestor of this idea. But Hegel adds what Kuhn notoriously lacked: an account of why the new paradigm is *better*, namely that it is what the old one turned out to be when tested by its own standard, and contains the old one’s truth (determinate negation). Kuhn’s critics accused him of relativism; Hegel’s method is an attempt to describe radical conceptual change that is nonetheless rational and progressive.

@retell
Any examination of knowledge seems to need a standard that has not yet been justified. Hegel’s solution is that consciousness carries its own standard, because it distinguishes how the object is for it (its knowing) from how the object is in itself (truth), so it can compare itself with itself while we simply look on. When the two do not match, both change: the object consciousness took to be the in-itself proves to have been only its own conception, and so the standard itself is tested. This movement, in which a new true object arises, is experience, and the new object is what the old one turned out to be in being known. Consciousness does not see how the new object arises; we see it “behind its back”, and this makes the series of shapes necessary, so that the path to science is itself the science of the experience of consciousness.
