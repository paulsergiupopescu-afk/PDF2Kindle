@unit 09 | Introduction I: Against the instrument | 73-80
@title Introduction I: The Fear of Error, the Way of Despair, and Determinate Negation

@where
The Preface was written last and looks back over the whole system. The Introduction was written first, before the chapters, and looks forward: it tells you how the book will work. Many teachers recommend starting here.

@guide
**The question this section answers.** Shouldn’t philosophy begin by examining our capacity to know, before it claims to know anything? And if not, how can it justify itself without simply asserting its own superiority?

**What Hegel does.** He starts with what seems “a natural idea”: before knowing the absolute, test the tool of knowing (¶73). This is the programme of Locke and, above all, of Kant. Hegel argues that the idea defeats itself: if knowing is an *instrument*, it alters its object; if it is a *medium*, it refracts it; and correcting for the distortion would just bring us back to where we started. Worse, the fear of error rests on unexamined assumptions, above all that knowing stands on one side and the absolute on the other: this “fear of error” is really “fear of truth” (¶74–75). But science cannot just dismiss rival views either, since “one bare claim is worth just as much as another” (¶76). The only way is to present *knowledge as it appears* and let it examine itself: the path of natural consciousness to true knowledge (¶77). For consciousness this path is “the way of despair”, a “self-accomplishing scepticism” far more radical than ordinary doubt (¶78). What keeps it from ending in nothing is *determinate negation*: each collapse leaves a determinate result and a new shape (¶79). The goal is fixed: where concept and object correspond (¶80).

**Watch for.** The **instrument** and the **medium**; “fear of error” as “fear of truth”; **bare claim**; **knowledge as it appears**; the **way of despair**; **self-accomplishing scepticism**; **determinate negation**; the **goal**; consciousness doing **violence to itself**.

**How to read it.** All eight paragraphs matter. Read ¶73–74 with Kant in mind, ¶76 slowly (it explains the method of the whole book), and ¶78–79 twice. These paragraphs are the best key to everything that follows.

@map
thesis: Philosophy cannot first test its instrument of knowledge; it must instead present knowledge as it appears and let consciousness test itself, on a path whose negations are determinate and lead to a fixed goal.
target: The “critical” programme of Locke and Kant (examine the faculty of knowledge first), and the sceptic who sees only nothing in the result of criticism.
step: The natural idea: test cognition, as instrument or medium, before knowing the absolute; this leads to the conviction that the absolute is unknowable (¶73).
step: This fear of error presupposes much: an instrument or medium, a distinction between ourselves and cognition, and the absolute on one side, cognition on the other; it is fear of truth (¶74).
step: Distinguishing absolute truth from some other truth only shows that the key words have not been understood (¶75).
step: Science may not dismiss its rivals or appeal to hints of itself within them; one bare claim is as good as another; so knowledge as it appears must be presented (¶76).
step: This presentation is the path of natural consciousness to true knowledge (¶77).
step: For consciousness it is the way of despair: a self-accomplishing scepticism, unlike ordinary doubt or the resolution to think for oneself (¶78).
step: Its negations are determinate, so each yields a new shape; this secures the completeness of the series (¶79).
step: The goal is where concept and object correspond; consciousness drives itself beyond each limited satisfaction (¶80).
conclusion: The book will be the self-examination of consciousness, not a preliminary investigation of our faculties.
stakes: Whether epistemology must come before metaphysics; how to answer the sceptic without begging the question.

@friendly Testing the ruler with the ruler
Suppose you doubt that your ruler is accurate. How would you check it? With another ruler. But how do you know *that* one is accurate? With a third... You cannot step outside all measuring to see whether measuring works. Hegel liked to tell the joke of the scholar who refused to go into the water until he had learned to swim.

Hegel thinks this is the trap of the “critical” philosophers who say: first examine your mind’s power to know, then you may claim knowledge. To examine knowing, you must already know something, about knowing. So instead of testing knowledge from outside, he proposes to watch knowledge test *itself*: take each way of knowing at its word, follow it, and see whether it lives up to its own claims. That is what the whole book will do.

@c 73 | objection | hegel
why: Hegel opens with Kant’s question: before doing metaphysics, examine reason’s capacity for it. His dilemma is sharp. Either the instrument changes the object (Kant’s active understanding) or the medium bends the light (Kant’s forms of sensibility); either way, subtracting the distortion subtracts the cognition. Inwood notes that Hegel may also be glancing at J. H. Lambert, whose “phenomenology” of 1764 tried to correct for appearance like an optics of illusion.

@l 73 | absurd in its concept
The plan fails by its very idea, not by poor execution. If knowing is a means placed between us and reality, what reaches us is always the product of the means, never reality as it is.

@l 73 | like a bird caught by a lime-twig
Bird-catchers smeared twigs with sticky lime to trap birds. Knowing cannot catch the absolute by such a trick; the absolute would mock the ruse.

@l 73 | already with us and willing to be so
Hegel’s own conviction slips in: the absolute is not remote from knowing but already present to it. The whole book tries to earn this claim rather than assume it.

@l 73 | only the pure direction or blank space
Subtract the ray from what the ray brings and nothing is left but the empty path it travelled. Remove knowing from what we know and nothing remains to be known.

@g 73 | the absolute
Here simply “what in truth is”, reality as it is in itself, independently of our knowing it. Hegel deliberately uses a neutral term at the start.

@g 73 | instrument
The picture of knowing as a tool applied to things (as Kant’s understanding applies its categories). Its counterpart is the *medium*, the picture of knowing as a lens or glass through which reality reaches us.

@u 73 | Why knowledge cannot be tested in advance
step: The critical philosopher reasons: we might be using the wrong faculty, or one with limits we do not see; so first examine the faculty. Hegel shows where this leads. If knowing is a tool, it reshapes its object; if it is a medium, it refracts it. Either way, what we get is not the thing in itself. Could we learn how the tool or medium works and subtract its effect? Then we would be left with the object untouched by knowing, which is to say, not known at all. The programme ends in the conviction that a boundary divides us from the absolute.

example: Imagine photographing a landscape through tinted glass. You could correct the colours if you could see the landscape without the glass to compare. But if *all* your seeing comes through the glass, you have no uncorrected view to compare with, and “subtracting the tint” is meaningless. Kant drew the conclusion: we know only appearances, never things in themselves.

misreading: Hegel is not saying that our cognition is perfect, or that we never make errors. He is attacking one *picture* of cognition, as a device standing between us and reality, and the programme of testing that device before using it.

returns: Hegel’s alternative is set out in ¶81–85: consciousness contains its own standard, so it can be examined from within. The image of a “beyond” cut off from knowledge returns in the Understanding’s “supersensible world” behind appearances (¶144–147), a beyond that turns out to be empty.

@c 74 | argument | hegel
why: The decisive turn: the cautious critic is not cautious enough. His “presuppositionless” starting point rests on a whole picture of mind and world, assumed without examination. Hegel turns the critic’s own weapon, suspicion, against him. The phrase “fear of truth” suggests that the critic’s modesty hides a wish not to be committed.

@l 74 | gets on with the job itself and actually cognizes
“Science” is Hegel’s word for philosophy done properly. Science simply gets on with knowing; the critic of ¶73 interrupts it with a worry: what if our tool of knowing is faulty? That worry “injects a mistrust” into the work before it starts.

@l 74 | this fear of erring is really the error itself
Hegel turns the critic’s own weapon round. If we must mistrust knowing before we use it, why not also mistrust the mistrust? Perhaps the fear of making mistakes is itself the mistake. The next two sentences show why it is.

@l 74 | needs to be examined first to see if it is truth
The irony: the critic demanded that nothing be accepted without examination, yet his own worry rests on assumptions he has not examined at all. His starting point is not presuppositionless; it takes “a great deal” for granted.

@l 74 | a distinction between ourselves and this cognition
The first hidden assumptions. To fear that the tool is faulty, you must already picture knowing as a tool or a lens (an instrument or medium), and picture yourself as standing apart from your own knowing, like a user holding a device.

@l 74 | separated from the absolute, and yet is something real
The deepest assumption: the absolute, what truly is, sits on one side, and our knowing sits on the other, cut off from it, yet still counting as something real. Hegel will now show that this picture contradicts itself.

@l 74 | is nevertheless genuine
Here is the contradiction. If knowing lies outside what truly is, it lies outside the truth; yet the critic still treats it as genuine knowledge, good enough to examine itself with. He gives knowing a status his own picture denies it.

@l 74 | fear of truth
The verdict. A view that separates knowing from the real in advance makes truth unreachable before any inquiry begins. So what presents itself as caution about error is really an avoidance of truth: a way of never having to commit to anything.

@arg 74 | The fear of error is itself an error
P1: The critical programme says: do not trust cognition until it has been examined.
P2: Yet the programme itself assumes, without examination, that cognition is an instrument or medium, that we are distinct from it, and that it is separated from the absolute.
P3: A procedure that forbids unexamined assumptions while relying on unexamined assumptions undermines itself.
C: So the critical programme has no right to its starting point; its mistrust deserves to be mistrusted.
weak: Stern points out that Kant need not rely on a “natural assumption”: he might be motivated by evidence that reason fails (the antinomies, the endless disputes of metaphysics). Like someone who nearly drowned and now reflects on his swimming, the critic may have real grounds for caution. Hegel’s argument works against the *a priori* demand to test first, not against every critique of reason.

@c 75 | reply | hegel
why: The Kantian reply would be: we know appearances truly, though not things in themselves. Hegel suspects this “other kind of truth” is a consolation prize. His deeper point is that we do not yet know what “absolute”, “truth” or “cognition” mean; we cannot settle their relations before we have examined them.

@l 75 | the true alone is absolute
Hegel names the premise behind his verdict in ¶74: truth and the absolute go together. To be true is to grasp what really is. So knowing cut off from what really is cannot be true at all.

@l 75 | capable of some other truth
The obvious reply, and essentially Kant’s: our knowledge cannot reach things as they are in themselves, but it is perfectly true of things as they appear to us. Second-best truth is still truth.

@l 75 | some other kind of true
Hegel is unimpressed. “Beating about the bush” leads to a murky distinction between absolute truth and some lesser kind of truth that is nonetheless supposed to be truth. What could that second kind be?

@l 75 | first needs to be ascertained
The deeper point, and the bridge to Hegel’s method. We talk confidently about “the absolute”, “cognition” and “truth”, but we have not yet established what these words mean. Their relations cannot be settled before their meaning is.

@c 76 | argument | hegel
why: The methodological core of the whole book. Hegel faces the oldest sceptical problem: when two parties each assert their view, neither can win without begging the question (the sceptics called it *equipollence*). His solution is neither to assert science nor to argue against rivals from outside, but to present the rival views themselves, and let them show their own limits.

@l 76 | assuming the air of serious and zealous endeavour
The opening clauses lump together all pictures of a knowing “separated from the absolute”: they all come down to the instrument and the medium. Then a barb: such worries can serve as an excuse to avoid the hard work of philosophy while looking very serious and responsible.

@l 76 | nor need we struggle for answers to all this
Hegel considers simply refusing to engage. We do not have to answer these worries one by one. The next sentences weigh two ways of dismissing them.

@l 76 | could even be regarded as a fraud
First option: reject the whole vocabulary as arbitrary. People use big words (“the absolute”, “cognition”, “objective”, “subjective”) as if everyone knew what they meant. Hegel says this pretence could even be called a fraud.

@l 76 | to provide this concept
Why a fraud? Because pretending we already possess the concepts lets us dodge “the main Thing”, the real task of philosophy, which is to work out and supply those concepts. The meaning of “the absolute” is a result, not a starting point.

@l 76 | when science itself comes on the scene
Second option, with “more right”: ignore the worries altogether. They are an “empty appearance of knowing”, and once real philosophy is actually done they will simply evaporate, as superstitions fade when a science matures.

@l 76 | is itself an appearance
The turning point of the paragraph. When science first arrives, it too is only an appearance: one more claim on the scene, not yet developed or proven. “Appearance” here means coming on the scene, not illusion. So science cannot look down on its rivals from a height it has not yet reached.

@l 76 | its appearing
Two ways of saying the same thing. Either science “appears” because it shows up alongside other kinds of knowledge, or those other, untrue kinds are science’s own appearing, science in an undeveloped form. Either way, science has not yet established itself.

@l 76 | only by turning against it
So science must free itself from being a mere appearance, and it can do so only by engaging with the other forms of knowledge, not by ignoring them. The rest of the paragraph shows that the two easy ways of doing this both fail.

@l 76 | of no account whatever
Easy way one: dismiss ordinary knowledge as a vulgar point of view, with science as a completely different kind of knowing for which it does not count.

@l 76 | the intimation of something better
Easy way two: point to hints of science inside ordinary knowledge (“deep down you are already on the way to my position”). Hegel rejects this too, a few lines below.

@l 76 | declaring its Being to be its power
Why the first way fails. Dismissing a rival amounts to saying “I am right because I am here and say so”: making one’s bare existence into one’s authority.

@l 76 | worth just as much as another
The rival can do exactly the same: “I exist too, and for me your science counts for nothing.” Two bare assertions cancel each other out. The ancient sceptics called this standoff *equipollence*, equal weight on both sides, and Hegel accepts their point.

@l 76 | how it is in and for itself
Why the second way fails, for two reasons. Pointing to hints of science is again just pointing to something that exists, another assertion. And it appeals to science in its weakest form, as it shows up within a defective view, rather than to science as it fully is: “in and for itself”, in its developed truth.

@l 76 | is to be undertaken here
The conclusion of the argument, and the reason the *Phenomenology* exists. Since science can neither dismiss its rivals nor claim them as secret allies, it must take them seriously, present them as they appear, and let them show their own limits. That presentation is the book you are reading.

@g 76 | bare claim
German *trockenes Versichern*, literally “dry assuring”: asserting something without grounds. Two opposed assurances cancel each other out, a principle of ancient scepticism.

@g 76 | knowledge as it appears
German *das erscheinende Wissen*: knowledge in the forms in which it shows up for consciousness, before it is fully developed. The phrase defines the subject matter of the *Phenomenology*.

@u 76 | Why science cannot simply win
step: Hegel’s problem: science, his philosophy, claims to be true knowledge. But when it appears, it is just one view among others. If it says “I am true and you are mere appearance”, the others can say the same back. If it says “there are hints of my truth in your views”, it appeals to something it would first have to prove. Either way it is one assertion against another. The only non-question-begging route is to take the other views on their own terms and show that they lead, by their own logic, beyond themselves, until they arrive at science.

example: Two friends disagree about whether a third friend can be trusted. Shouting “I know I’m right” settles nothing. The only fair way forward is for one of them to start from the other’s own beliefs and show, step by step, what follows from them. If those beliefs, taken seriously, lead to the first friend’s conclusion, the disagreement is resolved without anyone simply giving in.

misreading: This does not mean that Hegel is neutral about truth, or that all views are equally valid. He is confident that science is true. His point concerns *justification*: science must earn its standing by showing that other views lead to it, not by announcing it.

returns: Houlgate stresses that this gives natural consciousness a real right (compare ¶26) and makes the book an *immanent* critique. The method is spelled out in ¶81–87, where consciousness turns out to carry its own criterion.

@c 77 | definition | hegel
why: The famous description of the book as a journey, a *Bildungsroman* of the soul, with “stations” (an echo of the Stations of the Cross, and of the stages of a pilgrimage). Note the key phrase *natural consciousness*: ordinary, pre-philosophical awareness, which the book will lead, by its own experience, to spirit.

@l 77 | moving in its own peculiar shape
Because the book deals with knowledge as it appears, it does not look like philosophy in its finished form. Hegel means the system proper, above all the *Logic*, where thought moves freely in pure concepts. Here it must follow consciousness’s experience instead.

@l 77 | pressing on to true knowledge
So the book can be read as a journey: ordinary consciousness making its way towards real knowledge. The image of a path will return constantly (the “way of despair”, ¶78).

@l 77 | stations prescribed for it by its own nature
“Stations” suggests the stages of a pilgrimage, and perhaps the Stations of the Cross, each a place of suffering on the way to redemption. They are prescribed “by its own nature”: the route is set by consciousness itself, not by Hegel’s choice.

@l 77 | what it is in itself
The goal of the pilgrimage: to be purified into *spirit* and, by experiencing itself completely, to learn what it really is. The journey to true knowledge of the world turns out to be a journey of self-knowledge.

@g 77 | natural consciousness
Ordinary, pre-philosophical awareness of a world of objects, which takes its knowledge for real knowledge. The protagonist of the book’s journey.

@c 78 | argument | hegel
why: A key paragraph. Hegel contrasts his scepticism with two others: Descartes’s methodical doubt, which doubts in order to return to secure certainty, and Kant’s Enlightenment motto “dare to think for yourself”, which may keep all the old prejudices, now held proudly as one’s own. The way of despair is lived, not resolved upon: consciousness undergoes the collapse of its own certainties.

@l 78 | knowledge that is not real
Natural consciousness has the idea of knowing, it means to know, but not the reality: what it takes to be knowledge will turn out not to be. “Concept” here means a plan or germ not yet realized, as an acorn is the concept of an oak.

@l 78 | it loses its truth
Consciousness takes itself to know already, so each step that shows it otherwise feels like a loss. What is really progress (its concept being realized) it experiences as losing itself, because it identified itself with the certainties it is losing.

@l 78 | the way of despair
Not just doubt but despair. In German the step is from *Zweifel* to *Verzweiflung*, both built on *zwei*, “two”: being torn between possibilities, then being undone by it. See the Translation Lens after this paragraph.

@l 78 | as it was in the first place
Ordinary doubt shakes a belief for a while and then returns to it, so that everything ends up as it was. That is roughly Descartes’s method: doubt everything in order to get one’s beliefs back on firmer ground. Hegel’s path does not return home.

@l 78 | only the unrealized concept
What the path really is: a conscious recognition that knowledge as it appears is untrue, because what natural consciousness treats as the highest reality is only the unrealized concept, the plan of knowledge, not knowledge itself.

@l 78 | self-accomplishing scepticism
Scepticism that carries itself out: not a critic applying doubt from outside, but consciousness itself discovering, shape by shape, the untruth of its own certainties.

@l 78 | regard only one’s own deed as the true
The Enlightenment resolution: never accept anything on authority, examine everything yourself, follow your own conviction. Compare Kant’s motto “Dare to use your own understanding!” (1784). “Produce everything oneself… one’s own deed” probably glances at Fichte, for whom all knowledge begins from the I’s own act.

@l 78 | education of consciousness itself to science
Hegel’s path is a process of formation (*Bildung*), a history of how consciousness is educated up to the standpoint of philosophy, stage by stage. This is why the book is often compared to a novel of education.

@l 78 | this path is the actual performance
A resolution treats education as done the moment you decide on it (“from now on I will think for myself”). Hegel’s path is the long actual work, not the decision to do it.

@l 78 | replace error with truth
Thinking for yourself is better than obeying authority, Hegel grants. But switching from believing something because you were told to believing it because you are convinced may leave the belief exactly as false as before.

@l 78 | the vanity that goes with the latter policy
A sharp line: the only difference may be vanity, the pride of holding your prejudices as your own.

@l 78 | to investigate what the truth is
By contrast, a scepticism aimed at the whole range of consciousness, all its natural ideas and certainties at once, is what first makes a genuine inquiry into truth possible.

@l 78 | incapable of the task it proposes to undertake
Why? Because it produces despair in all our natural ideas, whether we call them our own or borrowed. Whoever starts investigating “right away”, as Descartes or the Enlightenment thinker does, is still full of those ideas, and so is not really free to investigate.

@lens 78 | Zweifel and Verzweiflung: doubt and despair
Hegel moves from *Zweifel*, doubt, to *Verzweiflung*, despair. Both words contain *zwei*, “two”: to doubt is to be divided between two possibilities, and to despair is to be utterly undone by that division. The step from one word to the other is the step from a method to an ordeal. Ordinary doubt is something I *do*, at a safe distance; despair is something that *happens to me*, and from which I do not return unchanged.

Hegel also calls the path *sich vollbringender Skeptizismus*, “self-accomplishing” or “self-completing scepticism”. It is not a sceptic’s attitude applied to consciousness from outside: consciousness itself carries out the doubt, as each shape discovers its own untruth. Compare the “labour of the negative” in the Preface (¶19, ¶32).

@u 78 | The way of despair is not Cartesian doubt
step: Descartes doubted everything he could, in order to find one certainty (“I think, therefore I am”) and rebuild knowledge on it; at the end, most of his old beliefs returned, now secured. Kant’s Enlightenment urged us to think for ourselves rather than accept authority. Hegel finds both too easy. Cartesian doubt is a resolution taken at the start; it leaves the doubter in control and expects to get the old world back. Thinking for oneself may simply mean holding the same prejudices with more vanity. Hegel’s scepticism is directed at the whole range of natural consciousness and is carried out by consciousness itself, which loses its truth step by step and does not get it back as it was.

example: There is a difference between deciding to question your political views, while privately expecting to end up where you started, and living through an experience that shatters them: the loss of a cause, a betrayal, a war. The second is closer to Hegel’s “despair”: the views do not survive, and what replaces them is something you could not have chosen in advance.

misreading: “Despair” does not mean the book is gloomy, or that it ends in hopelessness. For *us*, the readers, each despair is also progress (¶79). The despair belongs to natural consciousness, which experiences the loss of each certainty as the loss of itself.

returns: The most dramatic despair in this edition is the bondsman’s fear of death, in which “everything solid” is shaken (¶194). Scepticism appears as a shape of consciousness in ¶202–206, where Hegel contrasts it with the self-accomplishing scepticism of the book.

@c 79 | argument | hegel
why: The single most important methodological idea of the book: *determinate negation*. When a view fails, the way it fails is specific, and that specificity points to what must come next. This is also Hegel’s claim that the series of shapes is complete: each one generates its successor. Inwood argues that determinate negation can show that some successor follows, but not that only *one* could.

@l 79 | the necessity of the progression and interconnection itself
Hegel claims that the book will cover all the forms of untrue knowing, without drawing up a list in advance. The completeness will follow from the way each form leads necessarily to the next.

@l 79 | a merely negative movement
To see how, note first that the journey is not just a string of refutations, one view knocked down after another.

@l 79 | will present itself there
Seeing only destruction is the one-sided view natural consciousness takes. A position that makes this its whole outlook is itself one of the shapes on the journey: Scepticism, which appears in Chapter IV (¶202–206).

@l 79 | the nothing of that from which it results
What such scepticism forgets: when a view collapses, what remains is not nothing in general but the failure of that particular view, which tells us something definite. “Not this road” helps only if you know which road failed, and why.

@l 79 | has a content
So the result of a refutation is a “determinate nothing”: a nothing with a content, the lesson of the specific failure.

@l 79 | cast it into the same empty abyss
The pure sceptic, holding nothing but emptiness, cannot move. He can only wait for the next view to turn up and throw it into the same abyss, endlessly.

@l 79 | a new form has thereby immediately arisen
Understood as determinate negation, the failure of one view already contains the outline of the next: the new form arises at once from the specific way the old one broke down.

@l 79 | ensues of itself
This is how the series moves “of itself”: no outside push is needed, because each negation is already the transition. That is Hegel’s ground for claiming the series is complete.

@g 79 | determinate negation
The negation of a definite position, which yields not mere nothing but a definite result: the content of what failed, transformed. The motor of the *Phenomenology*.

@arg 79 | From determinate negation to a complete series
P1: Each shape of consciousness is shown to be untrue by its own experience.
P2: The result of this negation is not nothing, but the nothing *of that particular shape*: a determinate negation with a content.
P3: This determinate result is itself a new shape of consciousness.
P4: So each shape gives rise, of itself, to its successor, and no step is skipped.
C: The series of shapes is necessary and complete.
weak: P3 and P4 are disputed. A negation may be determinate and still leave several possibilities open (Inwood: “God is not yellow” leaves many options). And even a necessary chain from the first shape would not show that no other shapes exist outside it. Forster and Stern defend weaker versions: each transition is motivated by the failure of the last, without being the only possible one.

@c 80 | argument | hegel
why: Why does consciousness keep moving? Because, unlike a stone or a plant, it always has in view more than its present object: its own standard. So it is restless; every limited satisfaction is spoiled from within. Hegel lists the evasions of this restlessness, ending with the clever ironist who stands above every view; the target is the Romantic irony of Friedrich Schlegel.

@l 80 | no longer needs to go beyond itself
The destination is as fixed as the route: a knowledge that no longer needs to go beyond itself, because it finds itself in its object, where what it takes the object to be and what the object is fully coincide.

@l 80 | at any earlier station
So the journey is relentless: no earlier stage can give lasting satisfaction, because each still falls short of that goal.

@l 80 | this uprootedness is its death
A comparison with living things. A plant or an animal goes beyond its present state only when something outside forces it to, and for a living thing that uprooting means death.

@l 80 | consciousness is for itself its own concept
Consciousness is different. It always has in view its own concept, its idea of what knowing should be. So it goes beyond every limited state of itself from within, measuring where it is against where it should be.

@l 80 | as in spatial intuition
To know a limit is already to be beyond it, as in space: when you see a boundary, you also see the space on its other side. In grasping something limited, consciousness also posits what lies beyond it.

@l 80 | it spoils its own limited satisfaction
Hence the “violence” consciousness suffers “at its own hands”: every time it settles into a limited satisfaction, it spoils that satisfaction for itself by seeing beyond it.

@l 80 | what it is in danger of losing
Faced with this, anxiety may shrink back from the truth and cling to what it fears to lose. The rest of the paragraph lists three ways of clinging.

@l 80 | its restlessness disturbs indolence
First evasion: simply stop thinking. It fails, because thought keeps breaking in and its restlessness disturbs the comfortable rest.

@l 80 | insofar as it is a kind
Second evasion: sentimental tolerance, finding everything “good of its kind”. Reason replies that being merely one kind among others is exactly what makes something limited, so nothing is good just for being a kind.

@l 80 | the sole truth of vanity
Third evasion, the subtlest: a person who claims to love truth so passionately that no particular truth is ever good enough. The only truth left is their own cleverness: always cleverer than any thought they examine.

@l 80 | the barren I
This ironic vanity can dissolve any thought and, behind every content, finds only itself, an empty I. Hegel probably has in mind the Romantic irony of Friedrich Schlegel.

@l 80 | seeks only Being-for-itself
Hegel’s answer is to leave it alone: it flees from the universal, what can be shared and held in common, and seeks only its own self-enjoyment. It has stepped off the path, so there is nothing to argue with.

@g 80 | concept corresponds to the object
The goal of the journey: a knowledge whose idea of its object fits the object fully, so that there is no longer a gap between knowing and what is known. Hegel’s formula for “absolute knowing”.

@cross Is Hegel’s critique of Kant fair?
**Hegel’s case** (with Houlgate): Kant’s critical philosophy rests on an unexamined picture of cognition as a faculty with limits, standing between us and things. Hegel does not argue for an alternative picture here; he simply notes that this one is assumed uncritically, and so the critical philosophy is not critical enough.

**Stern’s reservation**: Kant had real reasons, the endless disputes and antinomies of metaphysics. A critique motivated by evidence of failure is not refuted by the swimmer joke. Hegel’s argument defeats the critical method only as a “natural assumption” prior to all inquiry.

**Heidemann’s reconstruction**: Hegel’s real problem is the ancient “problem of the criterion” (Agrippa’s trilemma): any test of knowledge needs a criterion that must itself be justified, leading to regress, circularity or dogmatism. Hegel’s answer, developed in ¶81–85, is that consciousness generates its own criterion.

**Your task.** After reading ¶81–87, decide whether Hegel has really avoided presupposing anything, or has only replaced Kant’s presuppositions with his own.

@obj Doesn’t Hegel presuppose something too?
objection: Hegel accuses the critics of presupposing a picture of cognition. But he presupposes that consciousness’s experience will lead somewhere, that negation is determinate, and that the goal is fixed in advance (¶79–80). These are large assumptions. If every starting point presupposes something, Hegel’s does too.

reply: Hegel can accept that the method must justify itself in practice, and that its claims about necessity and the goal are, here, only announcements (like the Preface’s “assurances”, ¶57). What he denies is that a *preliminary* examination of cognition could avoid presuppositions. His alternative is to presuppose as little as possible, the bare certainty of natural consciousness, and to let the experience show where it leads.

verdict: Is a method that is justified only by its results circular? Or is that how all inquiry works? See also Heidemann’s circularity objection in the next section.

@now Neurath’s boat
The philosopher Otto Neurath (1932) compared us to “sailors who have to rebuild their ship on the open sea, without ever being able to put it into dry dock and reconstruct it from the best components”. W. V. Quine made the image famous: there is no position outside all our beliefs from which to test them; we can only repair some while standing on others.

Hegel’s critique of the “instrument” is an early version of the same thought: there is no dry dock for knowledge. But Hegel goes further than Neurath. He thinks the repairs follow a logic, that each failed plank shows which plank must replace it (determinate negation), and that the voyage has a destination. Wittgenstein’s *On Certainty* offers another comparison: doubt itself presupposes certainty, so universal doubt is not a coherent starting point. Hegel would agree, and add that the certainties must then be tested from within.

@key
- The “natural idea” that cognition must be tested before use (Locke, Kant) defeats itself: an instrument alters its object, a medium refracts it.
- The fear of error presupposes much, above all a separation of cognition and the absolute; it is fear of truth.
- Science cannot win by assertion: “one bare claim is worth just as much as another.” So knowledge as it appears must be presented.
- The book is the path of natural consciousness to true knowledge, a journey through stations of its own nature.
- For consciousness it is the way of despair: a self-accomplishing scepticism, unlike Cartesian doubt or mere independent thinking.
- Determinate negation: each failure yields a definite result, a new shape; this is Hegel’s claim to necessity and completeness.
- The goal is where concept and object correspond; consciousness spoils its own limited satisfaction on the way.

@retell
Hegel rejects the natural-seeming idea that we must examine cognition before using it, since picturing it as an instrument or medium makes the absolute unknowable, and correcting for the distortion would cancel the cognition. This caution rests on unexamined assumptions, above all the separation of knowing from the absolute, so the fear of error is really a fear of truth. Yet science cannot simply assert itself against rival views, because one bare claim is worth as much as another; it must present knowledge as it appears and let it examine itself. For natural consciousness this path is the way of despair, a scepticism far more radical than ordinary doubt. It does not end in nothing because negation is determinate: each failure yields a new shape, until the goal is reached where concept and object correspond.
