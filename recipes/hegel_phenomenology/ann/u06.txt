@unit 06 | Preface VI: Against the schema | 50-57
@title Preface VI: Against the Schema: How a Science Must Organize Itself
@heading 50 | Against schematizing formalism

@where
Hegel has rejected mathematics as philosophy’s model (¶42–48). Now he turns to a newer, more fashionable fake of scientific order: the triadic charts of the philosophers of nature, which had become the house style of the Schellingian school.

@guide
**The question this section answers.** If not proof from axioms, what gives a philosophical science its order? And what is wrong with organizing everything by a pattern of three?

**What Hegel does.** He credits Kant with rediscovering *triplicity* (his tables of categories come in threes), but complains that it has been turned into a lifeless schema: thinkers explain an organism or the intellect by sticking labels on it such as “magnetism”, “electricity”, “north and south” (¶50–51). This “construction” is learned in fifteen minutes, like a doctor’s two-type theory of disease; it reduces the universe to a skeleton with paper labels, or a spice-seller’s rows of closed boxes. Science must instead organize itself “only by the concept’s own life” (¶53): the content becomes other to itself and then takes this otherness back, giving itself its own place in the whole. This is possible because “Being is thinking” (¶54–55): every determinate thing is held together by a thought, and so contains the seeds of its own movement. Scientific cognition must therefore *surrender to the life of the object* (¶53), and logical necessity is the rhythm of the content itself (¶56–57).

**Watch for.** **Triplicity**; **construction**; the **two-colour palette**, the **skeleton** and the **spice boxes**; the **table of contents** that gives no content; “surrender to the life of the object”; **Being is thinking**; the **cunning** of knowledge; *nous*, *eidos*, kind.

**How to read it.** ¶51 is satire and fun to read. ¶53–55 are dense and important: they state Hegel’s alternative. Note that this is the passage that disposes of the popular idea that Hegel’s method is “thesis, antithesis, synthesis”.

@map

target: The “formalism” of the philosophy of nature (Schelling’s school), which classifies everything by triads and polar analogies.
step: Kant rediscovered triplicity; it has been raised to its true meaning, but also degraded into a dead schema of predicates such as magnetism or electricity (¶50).

step: That excellence is so copied shows its power; the copying is its fate (¶52).

@friendly The spice-seller’s stall
Imagine a market stall with rows of small closed boxes, each neatly labelled: “cinnamon”, “clove”, “saffron”. The display looks orderly and complete; you can read off at a glance what is there. But you cannot smell or taste anything; the life of the spices is hidden in the boxes. Or imagine a skeleton with paper labels stuck on each bone: anatomy reduced to a chart, without flesh, blood or movement.

That, says Hegel, is what a schema does to the world. Think of the personality test that sorts everyone into sixteen types, or the consultant’s four-box diagram applied to every company. It feels like insight, and it can be learned in a quarter of an hour. But it never tells you why *this* person or *this* company is as it is. Real understanding has to go into the thing and follow its own life.

@c 50 | polemic | hegel
gist: Kant rediscovered triplicity, but only by instinct and still lifelessly; it has since been raised to its true meaning, so that the true form appears in its true content. Yet it is also degraded into a lifeless schema, reducing scientific organization to a chart: formalism thinks it has grasped a shape when it has pinned on a predicate from the schema, “subjectivity”, “magnetism”, “contraction”, “east or west”, and so on, in an endless circle in which each term explains the other and neither is understood.

why: Hegel defends the *real* use of triads (which he will make himself) against their mechanical use. The real use derives the three moments from the movement of the content; the fake use applies a ready-made pattern. Note the jibe at sensuous predicates that are “supposed to mean something other than what they say”: metaphor passed off as explanation.

@g 50 | triplicity
The pattern of three: Kant’s tables of judgements and categories come in groups of three, the third combining the first two. Fichte used the language of thesis, antithesis, synthesis; Schelling organized nature into triads of “potencies”. Hegel himself never used “thesis–antithesis–synthesis” for his own method.

@c 51 | satire | hegel
gist: Instead of the inner life of a shape, formalism offers superficial analogies and calls this “construction”. Like the medical theory of sthenic and asthenic diseases, it can be learned in a quarter of an hour. To say the intellect is electricity, or the animal is nitrogen, impresses the inexperienced with an appearance of genius, but shirks the real task of expressing the concept. It is a two-colour palette; it turns the universe into a skeleton with labels, a spice-seller’s boxes; and at the end, ashamed of its distinctions, it submerges them in the white of absolute identity.

why: Hegel’s most entertaining passage. Behind the jokes is a serious claim: classification by analogy is easy to learn, impossible to fail, and explains nothing. The final twist links this formalism back to “the night in which all cows are black” (¶16): the many-coloured chart and the blank absolute are two sides of the same external thinking.

@g 51 | asthenic, sthenic
Terms from the medical theory of John Brown (1735–1788), popular in Germany around 1800, which classified all diseases as caused by too little stimulation (asthenic) or too much (sthenic). Schelling’s circle admired it.

@u 51 | Two colours for everything
step: Hegel attacks a style of thinking that was fashionable among Schelling’s followers in natural philosophy. Its method is to take a few polar pairs (positive and negative, expansion and contraction, north and south) and a triadic pattern, and to fit every phenomenon into them by analogy. The intellect is “electricity”, the animal “nitrogen”, plants “the north”. Each label seems to unite remote things in a flash of insight; in fact it leaves both things unexplained.

example: Imagine explaining everything in the world as “yin and yang”: night and day, men and women, rest and action, salt and sugar. Each pairing has some suggestiveness, and the system can never be wrong. But you learn nothing about astronomy, biology or cooking. Hegel compares this to a painter with only two colours: red for history paintings, green for landscapes.

misreading: Hegel does not reject classification, analogy or triads altogether. He uses triadic structure constantly, and analogies (bud and blossom, acorn and oak) throughout the Preface. The objection is to a pattern imposed from outside and treated as explanation.

returns: The danger of formalism hangs over Hegel’s own method: readers will ask whether his transitions are generated by the content or by a pattern he brings. The Understanding’s “explanation” of lightning by electric force, which “explains nothing” (¶154–155), is a scientific cousin of this formalism.

@c 52 | claim | hegel
gist: Excellence cannot escape the fate of being stripped of life and spirit and having its skin worn by lifeless knowledge and vanity. But this very fate shows its power over hearts, and the universality and determinacy of form that make such superficial use possible.

why: A generous reading of the formalists: their copying testifies to the power of the original idea. Typically, Hegel finds in a degeneration the sign of something genuine.

@c 53 | thesis | hegel
gist: Science must organize itself only by the concept’s own life. The movement of what is consists in becoming other to itself, and then taking this unfolding back into itself, making itself a moment and a simple determinacy; so the content gives itself its determinacy and its place in the whole. The tabular understanding gives only a table of contents. Instead of surveying the whole from above, scientific cognition must surrender to the life of the object, immersing itself in it, and return into itself only as the content passes over into its higher truth.

why: This is Hegel’s positive programme. Two ideas stand out: the content *organizes itself* (it is a subject, ¶17), and the knower must *surrender* to it rather than stand above it. This is also the stance he asks of us in the *Phenomenology*: “simply to look on” (¶85), following the movement without imposing our views.

@u 53 | Surrendering to the life of the object
step: Hegel contrasts two attitudes. The “tabular” understanding stands above its material, surveys it and sorts it into a ready-made grid; it never sees the individual thing, only its place in the chart. Scientific cognition goes *into* the material and follows its own development: how one aspect leads to another, how the thing becomes other to itself and comes back. Only at the end, when the content has passed into its higher truth, does a view of the whole emerge, and it emerges from the content rather than being laid over it.

example: A biologist who knows a species only by its place in a taxonomy has a table of contents. One who has watched it live, feed, breed and adapt, and understands why its organs and behaviours fit together, knows the species. A good biography is not a timeline of dates but follows how a life unfolded from within.

misreading: “Surrender” does not mean passivity or uncritical acceptance. It is demanding work: it requires restraint from imposing one’s own categories and patience to follow the object’s logic. Hegel calls this “the strenuous effort of the concept” (¶58).

returns: Restated in the Introduction as the claim that we need bring no criterion of our own, since consciousness examines itself; our part is “simply to look on” (¶84–85).

@c 54 | argument | hegel
gist: Because substance is subject, all content is its own reflection into itself. A determinate thing subsists through its equality with itself; but equality with itself is pure abstraction, and abstraction is thinking. So “Being is thinking”. And because this equality is abstraction, the thing is also its own inequality and dissolution: its becoming. Knowledge therefore does not handle the content as something alien; it is the content’s own self, and watches with “cunning” as each determinacy, pursuing its own self-preservation, dissolves itself into a moment of the whole.

why: The metaphysical basis of the method: things can develop logically because they have the structure of thought. The famous phrase “Being is thinking” is not a claim that rocks think, but that whatever is determinate is held together by a thought-determination, which carries its own instability. Note the first appearance of the “cunning” of reason, here the cunning of knowledge.

@arg 54 | “Being is thinking”
P1: A determinate thing subsists only so long as it is equal to itself (if it were unequal to itself it would dissolve).
P2: Equality with itself, considered in itself, is pure abstraction: it abstracts from all the thing’s particular contents.
P3: Pure abstraction is the activity, or the product, of thinking.
C1: So the subsistence of a thing is a thought-determination: Being is thinking.
C2: And since abstraction from itself is also self-inequality, the thing contains its own dissolution and becoming; knowledge can follow this movement from within.
weak: P3 slides from “abstraction” as a feature of the thing (its bare self-identity) to “abstraction” as a mental act. Critics from Trendelenburg onward have charged Hegel with exactly this confusion of the order of being with the order of thought (see the Objections box).

@lens 54 | List: the cunning of reason
Hegel says knowledge is the *List*, the “cunning”, which seems to abstain from activity while watching determinacy, “just where it supposes it is pursuing its own self-preservation”, turn into its opposite. The word becomes famous later as *die List der Vernunft*, “the cunning of reason”, in Hegel’s lectures on the philosophy of history: individuals and nations pursue their passions and interests, and in doing so unknowingly serve the larger development of reason.

The idea is already here in miniature, and it also describes the reader’s position in the *Phenomenology*. Each shape of consciousness pursues its own certainty and, without knowing it, brings about its own transformation; “we” watch this happen “behind its back” (¶87).

@c 55 | argument | hegel
gist: The understanding has a significance for substance as it simply is: being-there is quality, determinate thought; so being-there is *nous*, which Anaxagoras first recognized as the essence, and which his successors called *eidos* or idea, that is, kind. Because being-there is kind, it is simple thought; its simplicity looks fixed, but it is also negativity, so it passes into its dissolution. Its otherness is in it; its movement is its own. The intellectuality of the understanding is a becoming, and so rationality.

why: Hegel locates his view in a long tradition: Anaxagoras’s cosmic *nous*, Plato’s forms, Aristotle’s kinds. Being is intelligible all the way down. The last sentence is a slogan for the whole book: understanding, with its fixed distinctions, turns of itself into reason, which grasps their movement.

@g 55 | nous
Greek: mind, intellect. Anaxagoras (5th century BC) taught that *nous* orders the cosmos; Socrates, in Plato’s *Phaedo*, complains that he did not use the idea to explain anything.

@g 55 | eidos
Greek: form, idea, kind. Plato’s word for the Forms, the intelligible natures of things. Hegel deflates it: the Idea “expresses neither more nor less than kind”.

@c 56 | claim | hegel
gist: It is the nature of what is to be its own concept in its being; in this lies logical necessity, the rationality and rhythm of the organic whole. The concrete shape, moving itself, simplifies itself into a determinacy and so raises itself into logical form. There is no need to impose formalism from outside: the content is itself the transition to form.

why: The answer to formalism: form is not added to content; it is content’s own becoming. This is what “speculative” means here: knowledge in which form and content are one. Inwood notes that Hegel elsewhere admits much in the world is contingent; logical necessity holds only at a high level of abstraction.

@c 57 | caution | hegel
gist: Scientific method is inseparable from its content and determines its own rhythm; its proper presentation is in speculative philosophy. What has been said is only an anticipatory assurance; it is not refuted by counter-assurances, familiar ideas, or new revelations of inner intuition. Resisting the new, or applauding it, are alike ways of protecting one’s own freedom and authority from what is unfamiliar.

why: Hegel ends with a warning to the reader: neither reject nor embrace what you have read so far without testing it. The real argument is the book itself. Note his shrewd psychology: both knee-jerk rejection and knee-jerk enthusiasm are ways of not learning.

@cross Is Hegel’s dialectic a formula after all?
**The textbook myth.** Generations of students learned that Hegel’s method is “thesis, antithesis, synthesis”. Gustav Mueller (“The Hegel Legend of ‘Thesis–Antithesis–Synthesis’”, 1958) showed that Hegel almost never used these words, which come from Fichte and Kant, and ¶50–51 attacks exactly that kind of schematism.

**The sympathetic reading** (Houlgate, Stern): Hegel’s “method” is not a pattern but the discipline of following what a concept or shape implies until it shows its one-sidedness. The resulting structures often have three moments, but because of how negation works (a position, its self-undermining, the result that contains both), not because a template is applied.

**The sceptical reading** (Kaufmann; Forster partly): the transitions in the *Phenomenology* are often looser than Hegel claims, and some look arranged to fit a plan; Forster thinks the text may need “reconstruction” to meet Hegel’s own standard.

**Your task.** In the chapters on Consciousness, count the steps of each experience. When there are three, ask whether the third really arises from the first two, or whether Hegel needed a third.

@obj Concepts are not forces
objection: Hegel says a thing “dissolves” because its self-identity is abstraction, and abstraction is thought (¶54–55). But logical negation (“A is not B”) is not real opposition (attraction and repulsion), and a concept being one-sided is not a cause of anything happening in the world. Trendelenburg and Lotze in the nineteenth century charged Hegel with confusing the order of thought with the order of being.

reply: Hegel’s point is not that concepts push things around causally, but that whatever is determinate is intelligible, and that a fully intelligible account of a finite thing must show why it is not self-sufficient: why it depends on others and changes. Inwood compares this to a genetic code embedded in a seed: the structure is not a separate force, but the form the process follows.

verdict: Is “the world is intelligible” enough to support “the world moves according to logical necessity”? Or does Hegel need a stronger, and more dubious, metaphysics?

@now Labels versus understanding
Hegel’s satire has a long afterlife. Personality systems that sort everyone into a handful of types, management frameworks that place every problem in a two-by-two grid, and grand theories that file all of history under one key word all fit his description: quick to learn, impossible to refute, flattering to the user, and silent about the particular case.

The contrast between “surveying from above” and “surrendering to the life of the object” also recalls later debates about method in the human sciences. Wilhelm Dilthey distinguished *explaining* (subsuming under laws) from *understanding* (following a life from within); ethnographers speak of “thick description”. Hegel would accept neither as a full account, but both inherit his insistence that knowledge must enter into its object rather than label it.

@retell
Hegel credits Kant with rediscovering the triadic form but attacks its degradation into a schema in which phenomena are “explained” by sticking on predicates such as magnetism or electricity. Such construction by analogy is easily learned, paints everything in two colours, and leaves a skeleton with labels or a table of contents without content. Science must instead organize itself by the concept’s own life, as the content becomes other to itself and returns, and cognition must surrender to the life of the object rather than survey it from above. This is possible because being is thinking: every determinate thing is held together by a thought, which also contains its own movement and dissolution. Logical necessity is thus the rhythm of the content itself, and all this remains an anticipation until the book carries it out.
